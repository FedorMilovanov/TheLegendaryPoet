## 0. Governing narrative

The second part is not the story of one fatal month and not a catalogue of scandals. It follows four years in which several processes converged:

- international fame and the public couple with Isadora Duncan;
- European and American travel, publication and alienation;
- return to a changed Soviet literary environment;
- the commercial and personal machinery of the “hooligan” role;
- sustained poetic work and unusually strong late texts;
- dependence, broken relationships and repeated attempts to reorganise life;
- worsening health and behaviour without retrospective diagnosis from poems;
- the documentary last weeks and the later construction of competing legends.

The article's central argument:

> Yesenin's last four years cannot be reduced to Duncan, alcohol, Imagism or the circumstances of death. The documents show a gifted and intensely productive poet who repeatedly recognised parts of his disorder, delegated the practical management of his life to others and still continued choices that deepened dependence, conflict and isolation.

The Christian/moral conclusion belongs to the end and to selected proven turning points. It must not replace the historical chain.

## 2. Chapter 1 — 1921: fame, group machinery and an inner fault line

### Function

### Opening scene

Use one documented public evening, leaflet or publishing object from 1921, not an invented backstage scene. The reader should see the poet already famous, performing a role and working inside a collective publicity system.

### Core claims

1. By 1921 Yesenin was no longer a provincial newcomer; he had publications, a recognisable voice and a public audience.
2. Imagism supplied publishing, performance, cafe and scandal infrastructure but did not create his entire poetics or every destructive trait.
3. The public “hooligan” persona could be consciously deployed while real disorder and dependence also existed.
4. The meeting with Duncan belongs at the end of an already unstable period, not as the magical beginning of all later collapse.

## 17. Chapter 16 — After the legend: gift, responsibility and the reader's false hopes

### Function

Return the reader from the forensic ending to the whole person, poetry and moral meaning.

### Synthesis

1. Yesenin was more than the village boy, Imagist, Duncan's husband, tavern figure or dead poet in a hotel.
2. His last years contain remarkable work, practical intelligence and moments of self-knowledge.
3. The same record contains repeated harm, dependence, broken trust and resistance to durable limits.
4. Talent and sensitivity are gifts; they do not reconcile a person to God or excuse sin.
5. Artistic confession can expose the heart without changing it.
6. The project does not invent a secret final conversion and does not claim omniscience about the last instant.
7. The evangelical appeal is directed to the living reader: do not turn beauty, fame, love or self-expression into a saviour.

### Tone

- sorrow, not triumph;
- clarity, not euphemism;
- no melodramatic “cursed poet” ending;
- no sentimental promise that poetry redeemed everything;
- no assertion beyond evidence about the final spiritual state.

### Navigation

End with links/cards to:

- Part I;
- first meeting with Duncan;
- `Москва кабацкая`;
- future Benislavskaya article (only after publication; until then no dead link);
- future Imagism article (only after publication);
- selected poem/music routes.
