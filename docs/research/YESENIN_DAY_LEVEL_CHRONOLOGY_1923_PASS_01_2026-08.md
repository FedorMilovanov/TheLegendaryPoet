## 1. Narrative purpose

The year 1923 is the hinge between the foreign journey and the late Moscow period. It should not be narrated as one abrupt homecoming followed by inevitable collapse.

The documentary year contains several distinct lines:

1. the last New York weeks and continued literary work;
2. a qualified 3–4 February departure and months in Europe before Moscow;
3. post-return transformation of foreign impressions into prose and public speech;
4. new literary and personal relationships in Moscow;
5. the `Москва кабацкая` role and real cafe/legal documents;
6. two different late-year criminal/public controversies that later retellings often merge.

## 2. Status vocabulary

- `DOCUMENTED` — dated letter, autograph, police/legal document, account, poster or physical publication.
- `ACADEMICALLY-ESTABLISHED` — academic commentary reconstructs the event from named evidence.
- `QUALIFIED-INTERVAL` — competing academic dates require a range.
- `SYNCHRONOUS-PRESS` — contemporary report; reception evidence, not automatic neutral fact.
- `MEMOIR-MAPPED` — named later witness, not a transcript.
- `RELATIONSHIP-RANGE` — personal contact supported over a span but not daily.
- `HOLD` — insufficient for exact reader-level statement.

## 6. Narrative consequences for Part II

### Return is a phase, not a scene

The chapter sequence after 3 August should be:

1. return and continuing Duncan correspondence;
2. rapid composition/publication of `Железный Миргород`;
3. 21 August public re-entry and polemic;
4. Miklashevskaya and `Любовь хулигана`;
5. Benislavskaya as emerging practical support;
6. cafe economics and real legal incidents;
7. November public/political case;
8. late-year hospital/literary context.

### Productive work and disorder coexist

1923 contains strong prose, public readings and a major lyric cycle while also containing alcohol, arrests and legal files. Neither line cancels the other. Productivity is not evidence of recovery; disorder is not proof that every work is an uncontrolled diary.

### Duncan does not disappear on 3 August

The 14 and 29 August nodes and later letters show a relationship moving through physical separation and correspondence. Avoid a cinematic “arrival, quarrel, final break” scene unless a specific document establishes it.

## 7. Standing anti-regression rules from 1923

1. **A departure date may require an interval.** Preserve `3–4 February` until transport evidence closes it.
2. **Foreign subject does not mean foreign composition.** `Железный Миргород` is dated in Moscow after return.
3. **A public report is not a formal lecture transcript.** The 21 August “report” was a wide-ranging conversation and performance.
4. **Manuscript and newspaper text are not identical.** Editorial omissions and staged submission must be mapped.
5. **A poem cycle is not a day-by-day diary.** Printed order does not establish composition sequence.
6. **A cafe bill proves an account, not consumption by one person.** Signatures, notes and ownership context matter.
7. **Two legal cases remain two cases.** September cafe case and November four-poets case must never be fused.
8. **A relationship begins as a sequence, not one totalising date.** Benislavskaya's later authority and Miklashevskaya's poetic role develop over time.
9. **A medical memoir can preserve a reading but misdate a final text.** Do not redraft PSS chronology from one late memory.
10. **Public return does not equal private resolution.** Duncan correspondence continued after Moscow arrival.
